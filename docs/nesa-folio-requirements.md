# The Major Textiles Project Folio — NESA Requirements and Recommendations Checklist

HSC Textiles and Design (NESA, Stage 6) · Major Textiles Project supporting documentation · Focus area: Costume

**How to use this document.** This is a requirements-only reference. It contains **no portfolio content** — it is the checklist the final text document is verified against before submission. It is ordered exactly as NESA's Major Textiles Project marking guidelines order the sections, which is also the order the folio must be physically presented in. For each section it gives: what NESA requires, item by item; the reasoning and the marking criteria behind each item; and NESA's or markers' recommended approach. Official source documents are listed in Part E.

---

# PART A — PROJECT STRUCTURE, MARKS AND PAGE LIMITS

## A.1 What is marked and what it is worth

The Major Textiles Project is worth **50 marks**: **25 for the textile item(s)** and **25 for the supporting documentation (the folio)**. The folio marks split as follows, and this is also the mandated order of presentation:

| Order | Section | Marks | Page limit (A3) | Page limit (A4) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Design Inspiration | 5 | 2 | 4 |
| 2 | Visual Design Development | 5 | 3 | 6 |
| 3 | Manufacturing Specification | 5 | 3 | 6 |
| 4 | Investigation, Experimentation and Evaluation | 10 | 4 | 8 |

- Maximum total: **12 A3 pages or 24 A4 pages, printed on one side only**. One "page" means one side.
- Use **either A3 or A4 consistently** throughout — not a combination.
- **Markers will not mark pages beyond the limit for each section.** Anything past a limit is invisible to the mark, so every page must earn its place.
- Per NESA's course advice, **title and contents pages are counted toward the limits if included** — confirm the treatment of any cover/title page against the current course advice (Part E, ref 2) and with the supervising teacher before adding one.

Note the weighting: Investigation, Experimentation and Evaluation is worth as much as any two other sections combined (10 of 25). Distribute effort accordingly — this is the most common under-investment in student folios.

## A.2 How the textile item is marked (the other 25 marks)

The folio must evidence the item criteria; the two halves must corroborate each other. The item is marked on:

- Appropriateness of design — aesthetic **and** functional aspects, suited to the focus area and end-use.
- Level of creativity and/or innovation, **resulting from the relationship to the decision-making process** shown in the folio.
- Degree of difficulty of the item.
- Proficiency and quality of manufacture.
- Evidence of management of the project to completion.

The folio is the evidence trail that makes the item's difficulty and decision-making visible to a marker who was not in the room.

## A.3 Rules that apply to every page

- [ ] **No student name anywhere.** The project is identified by NESA student number only.
- [ ] **Font no smaller than the equivalent of 12-point Times New Roman** for all text.
- [ ] **Font style and colour clear, legible and distinct from the background** — no ornamental display fonts for body text, no distracting backgrounds, no low contrast.
- [ ] Theme/colour scheme is permitted but **must never compete with legibility**.
- [ ] **Nothing that must be unfolded, flipped, opened or unwrapped.** Markers cannot manipulate inserts; every page must communicate flat, as it sits.
- [ ] **Every image numbered and referenced in the body text.** An image the writing never points to is decoration, and decoration is not marked.
- [ ] All physical samples and swatches **mounted flat and labelled**.
- [ ] Sections presented **in NESA's mandated order** (A.1).

## A.4 Cover sheet, identification and packaging

- [ ] The **Major Textiles Project Cover Sheet** (NESA paperwork) is completed and placed at the front of the folio on a separate page — leave the first sleeve of a display folio empty for it if the school inserts it in class.
- [ ] **Any outsourcing or external assistance is acknowledged on the Cover Sheet** — e.g. designer, fashion school, digital printing of fabric, pleating, professional machine quilting.
- [ ] Folio and item submitted together in one package: **maximum volume 0.2 m³, no side exceeding 1.2 m, lightweight material** (e.g. 1 m × 1 m × 0.2 m, or 1.2 m × 0.5 m × 0.33 m).
- [ ] The box is **not taped or wrapped** — tags and NESA paperwork are packed in class.

## A.5 Voice and form (recommended)

The folio is written in the **first person singular, in an analytic register**. The marker reads for evidence of *decision-making*, so the strongest sentence shape in the entire folio is cause-and-effect: *"[observed result]. **As a result, I** [decision taken in the project]."* Each section has its own register:

- **Design Inspiration** — past tense for the research, present tense for the design. Every influence claimed must land on a specific, visible feature of the item.
- **Visual Design Development** — the language of the **elements and principles of design** (line, shape, colour, texture, proportion, balance, harmony, contrast, emphasis, rhythm, unity, movement). Analysis, not description.
- **Manufacturing Specification** — the register of an industry specification sheet: precise, quantified, impersonal where appropriate; first person only for acknowledging and justifying pattern modifications.
- **Investigation, Experimentation and Evaluation** — the scientific register: Aim, Method, Results, Conclusion; controlled comparisons; quantified results; every conclusion feeding a decision back into the project with a cross-reference to where that decision appears.

## A.6 Recommended extras (unmarked, but they help)

- A **finished-item photograph "in action"** (worn/hung to show the end-use satisfied) as a stand-alone cover or end page. No face or identifying features; student number only. **Caution:** see A.1 — verify against current NESA course advice whether such a page counts toward a section's page limit before including it.
- A consistent running header/footer with student number, section name and "Page x of y" — orients the marker instantly and signals organisation.
- Cross-references between sections ("as costed on page 8", "as tested in Experiment 5") — they turn four separate sections into one continuous argument, which is what the top bands describe.

## A.7 Cut list — prohibited, unmarked or distracting

Remove or never include:

- [ ] **The student's name** — an identification breach, not just clutter.
- [ ] **Decorative borders, heavy backgrounds, ornamental fonts** — they cost legibility and signal effort spent in the wrong place.
- [ ] **Unreferenced mood-board images** — any image not numbered and cited in the text.
- [ ] **Generic history/culture essays** that never touch the item — every historical or cultural sentence must end at a design decision.
- [ ] **Diary or journal filler** — process narration without analysis attracts no marks.
- [ ] **Repeated text between sections** — say it once, cross-reference it elsewhere; page limits make repetition actively expensive.
- [ ] **Planning scaffolds** — to-do lists, teacher questions, word counts, notes-to-self, pagination markers.
- [ ] **Anything beyond the section page limits** — it will not be read.
- [ ] **Loose, foldable or 3D inserts** — markers cannot manipulate them.
- [ ] **Glued or painted decoration in place of textile techniques** — markers expect sewing and decorative *textile* techniques; anything attached should be attached by appropriate textile methods.

# PART B — SECTION-BY-SECTION REQUIREMENTS

---

## SECTION 1 · DESIGN INSPIRATION — 5 marks · 2 A3 / 4 A4 pages

### 1.1 A visual collage of inspiration, referenced in the writing

**Requirement.** Images, text and labels that show where the design came from — historical garments, cultural textiles, contemporary sources — with every image numbered and explicitly cited in the body text.

**Reasoning.** The criterion is about how inspiration is *communicated*, not how much was collected. A referenced image is evidence; an unreferenced one is wallpaper. Tying each image to the specific feature it authorised shows the marker the inspiration actually operated on the design.

**Marking criteria.** Top band (4–5): "clear communication… visual supporting materials" with the relationship to the focus area *clearly explained*. Middle band (2–3): "some visual materials included", techniques merely *listed*. The gap between bands is the referencing and the explanation, not the imagery.

**Recommended approach.** Four to eight images, each with a one-to-two-line caption stating what it is, its date/source, and the single design lesson taken from it ("read for…; adopted as…").

### 1.2 Relationship of the inspiration to the focus area

**Requirement.** A passage that names the focus area and explains how the inspiration serves that focus area's demands. For **costume**: character, narrative, historical setting, legibility at performance distance — rather than everyday fashion.

**Reasoning.** The focus area is the brief. NESA marks whether the design answers *that* brief; a beautiful garment that reads as daywear fails the costume focus area. The passage must argue the item's fitness for the focus area: what it communicates, to whom, from how far away.

**Marking criteria.** Top band: "the relationship of the design inspiration appropriate to the focus area must be clearly explained." Bottom band merely "identifies the focus area and briefly mentions the item".

**Recommended approach.** Open with the focus area, then three or four claims, each tied to a named, visible feature of the item.

### 1.3 Justification of creative and/or innovative design ideas

**Requirement.** A passage that names what is *new* in the design — the decisions that are genuinely the student's own — and justifies each one.

**Reasoning.** Creativity is a marked criterion on both the folio (here) and the item (creativity/innovation "resulting from the relationship to the decision-making process"). "Thoroughly justifies" means each innovation gets a *because*, not just an assertion.

**Marking criteria.** Top band: "thoroughly justifies particular creative and/or innovative design ideas." Middle band: ideas related to the focus area but merely listed.

**Recommended approach.** Name the central creative idea in one sentence, then isolate two or three decisions that are unambiguously the student's own and give each its because.

### 1.4 Critical analysis of historical, cultural and/or contemporary factors

**Requirement.** Analysis — not narration — of how historical and/or cultural and/or contemporary factors contributed to the **design and manufacture** of the item. At least one factor in depth; strong folios use two or three and show where they meet.

**Reasoning.** "Critically analyses and explains" is the top-band verb. Critical analysis traces every factor from source → feature → why it survived into the final design. It also legitimises the item's degree-of-difficulty argument (historically accurate construction, culturally specific fabrics and techniques).

**Marking criteria.** Top band: "critically analyses and explains the relationship" to historical/cultural/contemporary factors. Middle band: "few historical/cultural factors mentioned."

**Recommended approach.** One subsection per factor, each structured as: the source; the specific elements taken; the named features of the item they produced; one sentence of critique or synthesis (why this source over alternatives; where two sources meet in one feature). Include at least one sentence connecting inspiration to *manufacture*, not only design — the criterion says "design and manufacture".

### Section 1 checklist

- [ ] Every inspiration image numbered and cited in the body text.
- [ ] Each image captioned with source/date and the design lesson taken from it.
- [ ] Focus area named and its specific demands argued against features of the item.
- [ ] Creative/innovative decisions named and each individually justified.
- [ ] Historical, cultural and/or contemporary factors critically analysed, each landing on a named feature.
- [ ] At least one link from inspiration to *manufacture* (technique, construction method), not only to design.
- [ ] Within the page limit; no unreferenced imagery; no generic history essay.

## SECTION 2 · VISUAL DESIGN DEVELOPMENT — 5 marks · 3 A3 / 6 A4 pages

### 2.1 Freehand, labelled, high-quality sketches with logical development

**Requirement.** Hand-drawn design sketches — the suggested arc is three ideas plus a final — with a logical, sequential development between ideas: each design should visibly answer problems identified in the previous one. Front **and** back views for each idea wherever the item has a back worth showing.

**Reasoning.** The top band demands sketches that "clearly indicate the link between inspiration and design" in a "logical, sequential order". A set of unrelated pretty drawings scores mid-band at best; a chain where each design explicitly resolves named failures of its predecessors is the top-band shape.

**Marking criteria.** Top band (4–5): "appropriately labelled high quality sketches/drawings that clearly indicate the link between inspiration and design… evidence of creativity… logical sequential order." Bottom band: "poor unlabelled sketches lacking inspiration-design connection."

**Recommended approach.** Per design: front + back sketch, a numbered feature key, a short "modifications" list stating what changed from the previous idea and why, then the critical analysis (2.3).

### 2.2 Rendering that reflects the fabric; labels that classify features

**Requirement.** Sketches at a generous size, coloured, rendered to suggest the intended fabric (light wash or soft pencil for sheers; heavier rendering for structured cloth). Every labelled feature classified as **A (aesthetic)** or **F (functional)**; dual-purpose features marked **A + F**.

**Reasoning.** Fabric-appropriate rendering shows the design was imagined *in cloth*, not on paper. The A/F classification is the visible evidence of the "functional and aesthetic aspects" analysis the criterion demands — and it sets up the item criterion "aesthetic and functional aspects balanced". Dual-function features are creativity evidence.

**Recommended approach.** A key table beside each sketch: number · feature · A/F.

### 2.3 Critical analysis of each idea (elements and principles; strengths and weaknesses)

**Requirement.** For every design idea, critical analysis of aesthetic **and** functional aspects, using the elements and principles of design, covering strengths and weaknesses — a PMI (Plus / Minus / Interesting) table is the accepted format — connected back to the inspiration and end-use.

**Reasoning.** This is where "development" becomes marked evidence: the Minus column of one design should generate the Modifications list of the next. Analysis written in elements-and-principles vocabulary is what distinguishes "critically analyses" from "describes".

**Marking criteria.** Top band: "critically analyses the functional and aesthetic aspects" referencing design elements/principles. Middle band merely "describes".

### 2.4 The final design: front and back, coloured, labelled, and justified

**Requirement.** The final idea presented with front **and** back views (plus any accessory pieces), coloured, clearly labelled, and accompanied by a final written analysis justifying why this is the best possible design — referring back to the inspirations, the end-use and the earlier ideas.

**Reasoning.** The justification closes the loop: it must show the final design keeping what succeeded and removing what failed across the development, and it must argue fitness for the end-use. This is the single passage where the whole section's logic is tested.

**Recommended approach.** Feature key + modifications-from-pattern list + a compressed PMI + a final-justification paragraph structured as: why it beats each predecessor → how it answers the repeated criticisms → how it meets the end-use.

### Section 2 checklist

- [ ] Three development ideas plus a final design (or equivalent documented arc).
- [ ] Freehand sketches, generous size, coloured, rendered to suggest the actual fabric.
- [ ] Front AND back views for every design, plus accessory pieces where applicable.
- [ ] Numbered feature keys on every sketch; every feature classified A / F / A + F.
- [ ] Each design's modifications list explicitly answers the previous design's Minus column.
- [ ] PMI (or equivalent strengths/weaknesses analysis) for every idea, written in elements-and-principles vocabulary.
- [ ] At least some features explicitly linked back to numbered inspiration images from Section 1.
- [ ] Final design justified against predecessors, criticisms and end-use.
- [ ] Numbered leader lines still legible after any resizing for mounting.

## SECTION 3 · MANUFACTURING SPECIFICATION — 5 marks · 3 A3 / 6 A4 pages

### 3.1 Technical written description of the item

**Requirement.** An accurate, detailed, industry-style description of the finished item(s): garment type, fabrics and fibre content, colours, every structural feature, with measurements where they matter (closure lengths, tie dimensions, band widths).

**Reasoning.** The description proves the student can communicate a product the way industry does — precisely enough that a stranger could recognise every feature on the physical item. It is also the marker's map when examining the item itself: description and item must match exactly.

**Marking criteria.** Top band: "describes item(s) accurately and in detail." Bottom band: "basic description or pattern company listing."

**Recommended approach.** Two to three paragraphs, garment by garment, moving outside-in (silhouette → construction features → closures → embellishment), quantifying everything quantifiable.

### 3.2 Acknowledgement of patterns and description of modifications

**Requirement.** State plainly whether patterns are commercial or hand drafted, name and source them, and itemise every modification made, each with its reason.

**Reasoning.** Using commercial patterns is not penalised — concealing them is. The marked skill is the *modification*: evidence the student adapted a bought pattern to a designed outcome. A modification/reason table doubles as decision-making evidence for the item's creativity criterion.

**Recommended approach.** One sentence of acknowledgement (source and name of each pattern), then a two-column table: Modification · Reason, each reason pointing at the design development (Section 2) or an experiment (Section 4). Acknowledge any toile/mock-up made to establish modifications.

### 3.3 Production drawings — to scale, no colour, dimensioned

**Requirement.** Professional-standard technical drawings of the item as it sits flat: front **and** back views, drawn to a stated scale with a scale key, **no colour**, line only; **everything dimensioned** (panel widths, band heights, hem depths, lengths); seams, hems and topstitching indicated; seam and hem allowances stated.

**Reasoning.** These are trade drawings, not illustrations — the deliberate opposite of Section 2's rendered sketches. "Professional standard" is the top-band phrase; the scale key, dimensioning and allowance notes are what make a drawing professional rather than decorative.

**Marking criteria.** Top band: "drawings that clearly reflect the textile item(s) and that are of professional standard" with views, scale, proportions, complete dimensioning. Middle band: "elementary-standard drawings."

### 3.4 Pattern pieces with markings

**Requirement.** The pattern pieces drawn to scale with a labelling key: piece numbers/names, grainlines, folds, notches, cutting instructions ("cut 1 on fold", "cut 1 pair"); modifications to commercial pieces shown in a designated second colour or line style. Include pieces created for design features. A fabric-layout diagram or photograph keyed to a fabric key is strong supporting evidence.

**Reasoning.** Pattern literacy is the marked knowledge here: grainlines and markings prove the student understands why pieces are cut the way they are. Showing modifications in a second colour makes the "what I changed" claim from 3.2 verifiable at a glance.

### 3.5 Technical production plan — swatches, order of construction, notions and costing

**Requirement.** (a) Fabric and material swatches, each approximately 5 × 5 cm, mounted flat and individually labelled. (b) A step-by-step order of construction — complete but economical, aware of the page limit. (c) All notions itemised with quantity, unit cost and total cost in a table, including items from home (shown at no cost) and a grand total.

**Reasoning.** The plan proves the item was *manageable and managed*: real materials (swatches), a buildable sequence, and a real budget. The costing table also quietly evidences scope and independence. Check the arithmetic — markers do.

### 3.6 Product label

**Requirement.** A product label for **each piece**, containing all five fields: care instructions · fibre content · size (if applicable) · country of manufacture · brand name.

**Reasoning.** A completeness item — five required fields, easy full marks, and one of the most commonly dropped. Care instructions must be **consistent with the fibre evaluation in Section 4** (a fibre that cannot tolerate heat there cannot carry a hot-iron instruction here).

**Recommended approach.** One boxed label per garment piece plus, optionally, a swing tag; keep the brand name consistent with the folio's presentation.

### Section 3 checklist

- [ ] Technical description accurate, detailed, quantified, and matching the physical item exactly.
- [ ] All patterns acknowledged by name and source; commercial vs hand-drafted stated plainly.
- [ ] Every pattern modification itemised with its reason; reasons cross-reference Section 2 or Section 4.
- [ ] Production drawings: front and back, flat, line only, no colour, stated scale with key.
- [ ] Every drawing fully dimensioned; seams/hems/topstitching indicated; seam and hem allowances stated.
- [ ] Pattern pieces to scale with grainlines, piece numbers, cutting instructions; modifications in a designated second colour; keys provided.
- [ ] Swatches ~5 × 5 cm, mounted flat, individually labelled.
- [ ] Order of construction complete for every component, within the page budget.
- [ ] Costing table itemised with quantities, unit costs, totals; home items disclosed at no cost; grand total arithmetic checked.
- [ ] A product label per piece with all five fields; care instructions consistent with Section 4's fibre evaluation.

## SECTION 4 · INVESTIGATION, EXPERIMENTATION AND EVALUATION — 10 marks · 4 A3 / 8 A4 pages

### 4.1 Experiments across materials, equipment AND manufacturing processes

**Requirement.** Documented experiments relevant to this specific item, covering all three areas — **materials, equipment, manufacturing processes** — with **at least two experiments per area**. Each written up in the scientific format: Aim, Method, Results, Conclusion.

**Reasoning.** This is the highest-weighted folio criterion (10 marks) because it is the clearest evidence of the syllabus's core: informed decision-making. The three-area coverage forces breadth; the Aim/Method/Results format forces honesty (a fair test with a control, quantified results, and a conclusion the results actually support).

**Marking criteria.** Top band (9–10): "experiments extensively with materials, equipment and manufacturing processes applicable to the item(s). **As a result of experimentation modifies design and/or construction**… justifies their use on the basis of comprehensive investigations." Middle band (5–6): "some experimentation without clear application to design/construction." The band-splitting phrase is *as a result*: an experiment that changes nothing in the project is worth little, however well conducted.

**Recommended approach.** Per experiment: a title naming the decision at stake; Aim (to find/decide X for this item); Method (controlled, comparable samples, quantified where possible); Results (what happened, including failures); Conclusion beginning "As a result, I…" and cross-referencing the page where that decision now lives.

### 4.2 Result samples mounted in the folio

**Requirement.** The physical outputs of the experiments — stitched samples, sealed edges, test swatches, closure samples — mounted flat in the folio and labelled to match the write-ups.

**Reasoning.** Samples are the proof the experiments happened and the marker's only way to verify claims with their own eyes. They must sit flat: markers cannot unfold or manipulate. Where a result cannot be mounted flat (e.g. drape), a labelled photograph of the test is the substitute.

### 4.3 Justification of final choices from results

**Requirement.** Every conclusion justifies the choice made for the project *on the basis of the investigation* — not preference, habit or teacher advice.

**Reasoning.** "Justifies… on the basis of comprehensive investigations" is the top-band wording. The folio-wide payoff: modifications in Section 3 and features in Section 2 that each trace back to a numbered experiment here make the whole folio one argument.

### 4.4 Critical evaluation of the properties and performance of fibre, yarn and fabric against the end-use

**Requirement.** A written evaluation — distinct from the experiments — that critically assesses the fibres, yarns and fabrics used, in relation to the end-purpose: their properties (e.g. absorbency, thermoplasticity, drape, durability), how they performed, where they fell short, and what would change on repetition.

**Reasoning.** This is the syllabus's science of textiles applied to the student's own item. "Critically evaluates… in relation to the end-purpose" outranks "describes properties": the top band demands honest weaknesses and a considered alternative, not advertising copy for one's own fabric choice.

**Recommended approach.** Work through fibre → yarn → fabric structure, each level tied to an observed behaviour of the actual item; close with a summary table (component · fibre/yarn/fabric · property that decided it · care) and an "if I repeated this project" judgement.

### Section 4 checklist

- [ ] At least two experiments in each of: materials, equipment, manufacturing processes.
- [ ] Every experiment in Aim / Method / Results / Conclusion format, with a control and quantified results where possible.
- [ ] Every conclusion states "As a result, I…" — a decision that visibly changed the project — with a cross-reference to where the decision appears.
- [ ] Physical result samples mounted flat and labelled to match the write-ups (photographs only where flat mounting is impossible).
- [ ] Final choices justified from results, not preference.
- [ ] Fibre/yarn/fabric evaluation addresses properties AND performance against the end-use, admits genuine weaknesses, and proposes a considered alternative.
- [ ] Care claims here consistent with the product labels in Section 3.
- [ ] Two-experiments-per-area minimum never sacrificed to page pressure.

# PART C — FINAL SUBMISSION CHECKLIST (condensed)

**Folio.**

- [ ] Cover Sheet at front on a separate page (or first sleeve left empty for NESA paperwork per school practice); outsourcing/assistance acknowledged on it.
- [ ] Student number on every page; name nowhere.
- [ ] Font ≥ 12-point Times New Roman equivalent; high contrast; no distracting backgrounds.
- [ ] A3 or A4 used consistently; one side only; section page limits respected; sections in NESA order.
- [ ] Every image numbered and referenced; all samples and swatches mounted flat.

**Item.**

- [ ] All loose threads cut; facings secured; creases pressed out; hems, darts, seams and folds pressed.
- [ ] No unintended raw edges; anything not for the marker's eyes hidden neatly.
- [ ] Decoration attached by textile techniques, not glue or paint.
- [ ] Submitted in a box within 0.2 m³ / 1.2 m max side, lightweight, neatly packed with the folio, box NOT taped or wrapped; bubble wrap / packing paper as needed.

**Time.**

- [ ] Final assembly takes more than two days — schedule it.

# PART D — CROSS-SECTION CONSISTENCY CHECKS

These are the checks that turn four sections into one argument — the shape the top bands describe:

- [ ] Every feature claimed in Design Inspiration is visible on the final design in Section 2 and described in Section 3.
- [ ] Every modification in Section 3's table traces to a Section 2 analysis or a Section 4 experiment.
- [ ] Every Section 4 conclusion lands on a decision that appears in Section 2 or Section 3, cross-referenced by page.
- [ ] Care instructions (Section 3 labels) agree with fibre properties (Section 4 evaluation).
- [ ] The technical description (Section 3) matches the physical item exactly.
- [ ] No text repeated between sections — cross-reference instead.
