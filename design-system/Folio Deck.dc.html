The Sources, Gathered, Part I
The ledger. This folio documents a two-scene dance costume - a black satin dress with sheer sleeves, a tiered tulle overskirt that hooks off at the waist, and a white collar that snaps on - designed for a stage interpretation of Wednesday Addams at a school dance, and made to be read by an audience at performance distance rather than worn as everyday dress. The ledger is kept as an evidence trail: every feature claimed here is traced to a source on these pages, and every modification to a test.

2 Mourning dress, British, c. 1905. Read for the high fitted collar, the long sleeve and a bodice seamed close to the body.
3 Evening dress in black silk tulle, French, c. 1926. Read for tiers cut on the straight grain, equal in depth and set close together.

Relevance to the focus area

The focus area of my Major Textiles Project is costume. Costume establishes a character before dialogue or action, and that idea has driven every decision in this folio.

The character is Wednesday Addams, dressed for a school dance she did not want to attend. The dress she wears to that dance in the 2022 series (image 1) is my starting point, and the audience will know it. On a stage it has to read at performance distance under gym lighting: one black silhouette and a skirt that moves when she does. It would not be worn as modern daywear, and that is the point.

The whole scheme is black on black in two textures - a matte satin dress and sheer tulle tiers - so the audience reads shape and movement rather than colour. That is the screen dress’s own idea, and I kept it.

Images 2 to 4 are the three periods the screen dress quietly quotes. I went to each one to correct something in the original that fails on a stage: the 1905 dress closes the line, the 1926 dress regularises the tiers, and the 1955 dress fixes the length.

Creativity and innovation

The innovation is that the costume converts. The tiered skirt is a separate overskirt hung from a belt band that hooks at the side, and the white collar snaps on. Overskirt on and collar off is the dance dress. Overskirt off and collar on is the plain black dress the character wears every day in the same series. One garment serves two scenes, and the change takes under a minute in the wings (Experiment 8).

A screen costume is built for a camera and one scene. A stage costume has to survive a run and a quick change, so the invention is in the making: the tiers hang from their own carrier so their weight never sits on the dress, the band hides the join, and the dress is finished as a dress in its own right.

Three smaller decisions are also mine. The uneven tiers of the original are regularised and graded, 18, 26 and 34 cm, so the skirt widens towards the hem and moves in a delay when she turns. The ruffled cap sleeve becomes a long sheer sleeve with a satin cuff, closing the line as the 1905 dress does. The buckle goes, and the belt becomes the band that carries the overskirt.

Selected pages of this folio print in black and white, in the manner of the 1964 television series the character comes from. Where a plate is monochrome, that is a design decision, not a printing fault.

The Sources, Gathered, Part II
Screen source · Wednesday and The Addams Family

The starting point is the dance dress from the 2022 series (image 1): a sheer black layer over an opaque underdress, a ruffled yoke over a black shirt collar, ruffled cap sleeves, a wide buckled belt, and four uneven sheer tiers to mid-calf. It was built for a camera at close range and for one scene. I kept what the audience needs to recognise her - the black on black, the tiers, the belt, the length - and changed what fails at ten metres or in the wings.

The white collar (image 7) is the character’s uniform in the same series, worn every day. Putting it on the same dress as a detachable piece, with the tiers off, is what lets one costume play two scenes. The 1964 television series (image 6) was shot in black and white, and its costumes were designed to read in that range: matte black against mid grey against a hard white. That tonal discipline is the reason my dress has no colour at all, and the reason selected plates in this folio are printed in monochrome.

Historical influence · dress in black, 1905 to 1955

The screen dress quotes three moments when black dress was the height of fashion rather than its absence, and I went to each one to fix a fault in the original. Edwardian mourning dress (image 2) is a bodice seamed close to the body, a high collar and a long sleeve. Nothing is bare and the whole figure reads as one dark shape. It gave me the closed line and the long sleeve that replace the bare yoke and the cap sleeve.

The 1920s tulle evening dress (image 3) makes the same black move: tiers of sheer ruffles, equal in depth and set close together, so the skirt lifts and settles a beat after the wearer. It gave me regular tiers in place of the original’s uneven ones. The 1950s tea-length party dress (image 4) is a fitted bodice over a full tulle skirt that stops above the ankle so the wearer can dance in it. It fixed the length and the proportion of bodice to skirt.

Those corrections shaped the manufacture as much as the look: the dress and the tiers are made as two garments and joined at the waist by the band (page 8).

Technique influence · jet beading and horsehair braid

Victorian mourning bodices carried jet beading (image 5), black on black, so the surface caught light without introducing colour. I use the same idea in a small way: a line of black glass beads along the cuff seam, the only surface decoration on the dress.

Horsehair braid inside a ruffle edge (image 8) is how a tulle tier is made to stand without stiffening the whole skirt. It set the manufacturing method for the satin skirt hem and was tested against three other edge finishes in Experiment 7.

Critical analysis · plus, minus, interesting
Design Plus Minus Interesting
One
  • It is recognisably the character; the audience knows this dress before she moves.
  • Tea length and a full tiered skirt let the wearer dance, which is the end-use.
  • Two blacks, sheer over matte, carry all the interest without colour.
  • A black collar on black vanishes at ten metres, and the yoke ruffle fights it for the same spot.
  • A metal buckle flares under hall light, and a ruffled cap sleeve reads as sweet, which the character is not.
  • Four uneven tiers are hard to gather evenly, and a copy scores nothing for design.
  • It was built for a camera at close range, not for a hall.
  • Black and white, no colour at all, holds the 1964 reference as well as the 2022 one.
  • The belt is the one hard line on a soft dress.
Two
  • The closed line and high neck are exactly the mood of the character.
  • Jet beading gives a black surface depth without colour.
  • Long sleeves add direction and elongate the figure.
  • A floor-length column cannot be danced in; the back slit only allows walking.
  • Beading the whole bodice by hand takes the whole term and leaves nothing for testing.
  • A stand collar in satin is hot under lights and hides the neck, so there is nowhere for a white collar to go.
  • Matte satin absorbs light where a shiny satin would flare.
  • The design is all texture and no shape change: it stands still well and moves badly.
  • A mourning dress at a dance is a deliberately contrasting idea.

Final justification

This is the best of the four designs because it keeps every element that succeeded in the earlier ideas and removes each one that failed. The two blacks, the tiers, the belt line and the length of the screen dress are kept; the closed line and long sleeve of Design Two are kept; Design Three’s tiers are kept and graded so the skirt widens towards the hem and the eye is drawn down and out.

The overskirt answers the fault the other three share: each is one dress for one scene. Hanging the tiers from a band means the same garment is the dance dress with the overskirt on, and the school dress with it off and the collar on, in under a minute (Experiment 8). The band is the screen dress’s belt with the buckle removed and a job given to it: it carries the tiers and hides the join. The satin cuff finishes the sheer sleeve, and its single line of jet beads is the one place the surface catches light, a controlled version of Design Two’s beading. The invisible zip replaces the lapped zip because a ridge shows through matte satin under stage light (Experiment 9).

Against the end-use it does what a costume must: the audience recognises the character from the back of a hall, the graded tiers move when she dances, and the same dress walks on for the next scene. Every feature is traced to a source on pages 1 and 2 or to a test on pages 9 to 11.

Critical analysis · plus, minus, interesting
Plus Minus Interesting
  • Two blacks, matte and sheer, give contrast within one hue, creating unity.
  • Graded tiers give the skirt direction and movement.
  • Two scenes from one garment.
  • Raw-cut tulle snags on jewellery and chairs and cannot be mended invisibly.
  • Nylon tulle holds heat; the dress will be warm under lights.
  • The overskirt hangs on three hooks; if one fails on stage the skirt drops.
  • The band is all that survives of the original belt, and it is now the most functional piece on the dress.
  • The three periods are corrections to a 2022 dress, not a costume of their own; she reads as out of time without the dress being unplaceable.

The Making, Part I
Technical description

A two-scene black dance costume in three pieces: a dress in 100% polyester crepe satin, worn matte side out, with long sleeves in black nylon tulle; an overskirt of three tulle tiers on a tulle carrier, hung from a 40 mm grosgrain band that hooks at the left side; and a detachable collar in 100% cotton piqué.

The bodice is princess-seamed with a round neckline faced in self fabric and finished with four covered snaps for the collar. The sheer set-in sleeves end in a 4 cm satin cuff with a covered button and a line of 4 mm jet beads along the cuff seam. The skirt is four panels to 68 cm, hemmed over 25 mm horsehair braid. Centre back closes with a 40 cm invisible zip and a neck hook.

The carrier is one layer of tulle, 64 cm deep, gathered into the band: grosgrain over petersham, closed with three hooks and bars. The tiers, 18, 26 and 34 cm deep, are set at 0, 14 and 34 cm from the waist at two and a half times their length, edges raw. The collar is flat piqué, 7 cm deep at centre back, interfaced, on a stand carrying the four snap sockets.

Patterns and modifications

The bodice and sleeve are from a commercial princess-seamed fitted-dress pattern. The skirt, tiers, carrier, band and collar are drafted from my measurements, because no commercial pattern carried tiers at the depths I wanted or an overskirt that comes off. Every change below is a modification I made to the purchased pieces, and each is drawn in the designated second colour on page 7. The modifications were established on a calico toile before cutting.

Order of construction
Dress
  1. Trace the commercial bodice and sleeve; draft the skirt, tiers, carrier, band and collar.
  2. Sew a calico toile; fit and mark alterations.
  3. Alter the pattern: neckline +3 cm, waist shortened, sleeve narrowed.
  4. Lay out and cut satin and tulle; overlock all satin edges.
  5. Construct the bodice: princess seams, then side seams; press.
  6. Face the neckline; set four snap studs through the facing.
  7. Construct the skirt; hem over horsehair braid.
  8. Join bodice to skirt at the waist; insert the invisible zip.
  9. Set the sleeves; attach the cuffs and covered buttons.
  10. Bead the cuff seams through the cuff interfacing.
  11. Final press, satin only. Hook on the overskirt, snap on the collar, and check the fall of both scenes.
Overskirt
  1. Cut the carrier 64 cm deep at 1.5 times the waist; join the short ends, leaving 15 cm open at the top left.
  2. Cut each tier along the tulle at 2.5 times the carrier circumference; cut the lower edge on the finished line.
  3. Zig zag over a gathering cord along each upper edge (Experiment 5); draw up to the carrier and distribute evenly against the marks at 0, 14 and 34 cm.
  4. Stitch each tier to the carrier with a walking foot (Experiment 4), deepest tier lowest.
  5. Gather the carrier top into the band, 40 mm grosgrain over 38 mm petersham; three hooks and bars at the left, two covered snaps down the overlap (Experiment 8).
Collar
  1. Cut two collars and two stands in piqué; cut one of each in sew-in interfacing.
  2. Baste interfacing to the under collar and the inner stand.
  3. Stitch the collar pieces right sides together; trim, clip and turn; press.
  4. Understitch the under collar.
  5. Sandwich the collar between the stands; stitch and turn.
  6. Set four snap sockets in the inner stand at the marks.
  7. Edge stitch the stand closed by hand.
  8. Press with a warm iron; snap to the dress and check the fall.

March–April · Construction - bodice, skirt, zip, overskirt.
May–June · Sleeves, cuffs, beading and band.

Trials & Proofs, Part I
Area one · Materials
Experiment 1 · Tulle weight for the tiers
Aim
To find the tulle that holds a gathered tier away from the skirt, moves when the wearer turns, and does not scratch. Sample 1: soft nylon tulle. Sample 2: mid-weight nylon tulle. Sample 3: stiff bridal tulle.
Method
Three 100 × 16 cm strips were gathered to 40 cm and stitched to identical satin panels. Each was hung, photographed against a grid, spun by hand and photographed again at rest. Each was rubbed against the inside of a forearm for thirty seconds. Cost per metre was compared.
Results
Soft tulle collapsed against the satin and read as a fringe. Mid-weight tulle stood 4 cm off the panel, lifted on the spin and settled in about one second. Stiff tulle stood 9 cm off, did not settle, and left visible marks on the skin.
Conclusion
Only the mid-weight tulle gave a tier that stands, moves and can be worn against the arm. As a result, I selected mid-weight nylon tulle for the tiers, the carrier and the sleeves and ordered 7 m, as costed on page 8.

Experiment 2 · Collar fabric: holding a white edge
Aim
To find the white fabric that holds a clean collar edge, stays white against black satin, and survives washing without the interfacing showing through.
Method
Three 12 cm collar samples were made by the same method in cotton poplin, cotton piqué and polyester satin, each with sew-in interfacing. Each was snapped to a black satin panel, photographed under a warm light, washed three times at 30 degrees, pressed and photographed again.
Results
Poplin held its edge when pressed but creased across the front by the third wash. Piqué held a crisp edge through all three washes and its texture hid the interfacing entirely. Satin read as grey under the light, showed the interfacing as a shadow and rolled at the edge.
Conclusion
Piqué was the only fabric that stayed white, flat and crisp. As a result, the collar is cotton piqué over sew-in interfacing, and it carries its own care label because it is a different fibre from the dress (page 7).

Experiment 3 · Bodice fabric under stage light
Aim
To find the black fabric that reads as black, not grey or shine, under the lighting of a school hall, and that takes a princess seam cleanly.
Method
Four 20 × 20 cm samples - crepe satin shiny side, crepe satin matte side, cotton sateen and polyester crepe - were pinned to a board and photographed at 3 m and 10 m under the hall lights with the camera set the same for each. A curved seam was machined in each and pressed.
Results
The shiny satin flared white along every fold at 3 m. Cotton sateen read as dark grey at 10 m. Polyester crepe read as black but the seam puckered. The matte face of the crepe satin read as black at both distances, showed the seam as a line and pressed flat.
Conclusion
Only the matte face of the crepe satin gave a true black and a clean seam. As a result, the bodice, skirt and cuffs are crepe satin worn matte side out, as costed on page 8, and the shiny face is used only inside the cuff.

Experiment 4 · Presser foot for tulle over satin
Aim
To find the presser foot that feeds a gathered tulle tier over its tulle carrier, and a tulle sleeve into a satin armhole, without the top layer creeping ahead of the bottom or the tulle catching in the feed dogs.
Method
Three 40 cm tier seams were stitched with the same needle, thread and stitch length using the standard foot (control), a Teflon foot, and a walking foot. The start and end marks on both layers were measured for drift, and each seam was inspected for caught or torn tulle.
Results
The standard foot let the tulle creep 11 mm over 40 cm and caught it twice. The Teflon foot reduced the creep to 6 mm but still caught the tulle once. The walking foot fed both layers together with 1 mm of drift and no catching.
Conclusion
Only the walking foot fed tulle and satin as one. As a result, every tier seam and every sleeve seam is stitched with the walking foot, a step recorded on page 8.

Experiment 5 · Gathering method for the tiers
Aim
To find the method that gathers a 340 cm tulle tier to 136 cm evenly, without the thread breaking and without the gathers shifting while the tier is set.
Method
Three 100 cm strips of the selected tulle were gathered to 40 cm by two rows of machine basting (control), a ruffler foot set to one tuck in six, and a zig zag stitch worked over a cotton gathering cord. Each was measured at five points for evenness and then set to a carrier strip.
Results
The basting thread broke twice while drawing up. The ruffler gave even tucks but pleated rather than gathered and the tulle read as folded. The cord drew up without breaking, gathered evenly within 3 mm at every point, and held its place while the tier was stitched.
Conclusion
Zig zag over a cord was the only method that was even, strong and stable. As a result, all three tiers are gathered over cord, as recorded on page 8, and the cord is drawn out after the tier is set.

Experiment 6 · Marking tools on black fabric
Aim
To find the marking tool that shows on black satin and black tulle, survives handling, and leaves no trace after construction.
Method
Pattern lines were marked on 15 cm samples of both fabrics with white tailor’s chalk, a heat-erasable pen, tailor’s tacks in white thread, and a white water-soluble pencil. Each sample was handled for ten minutes, then brushed, pressed or sponged as the tool requires.

Chalk showed clearly on satin and brushed out; on tulle it fell through the net in minutes. The heat pen marked satin but left a pale ghost after pressing. Tailor’s tacks held on the tulle through handling and pulled out clean. The pencil showed on satin but needed water to remove.
Conclusion
As a result, I marked the satin with chalk and the tulle with tailor’s tacks only, and kept the heat pen away from the satin, as recorded on page 8.

Experiment 7 · Hem and edge finish, tulle and skirt
Aim
To find the edge finish for the tulle tiers that stays light and does not fray, and the hem for the satin skirt that holds the lowest tier away from the legs.
Method
Four 20 cm tulle edges were finished raw cut (control), rolled hem, satin bound, and overlocked. Four satin hems were finished as a double-turned 2 cm hem (control), a 3 mm rolled hem, a 2 cm hem over 25 mm horsehair braid, and a faced hem. All were hung 48 hours with a tier over them and photographed against a grid.
Results
The raw tulle edge did not fray at all and stayed the lightest. The rolled hem fluted, the binding read as a hard line, and the overlocking curled. On the satin, the horsehair hem held the tier 5 cm out; the double turn and rolled hem let it collapse, and the faced hem creased.
Conclusion
As a result, every tulle edge is cut raw on the finished line, and the skirt is hemmed over horsehair braid, both as specified in the allowances box on page 6.

Experiment 8 · Closing the overskirt band
Aim
To find the band closure that carries three tiers through a dance, opens in under a minute, and shows nothing at the waist.
Method
Four 40 mm bands were closed with 10 cm of hook-and-loop tape, four covered snaps, a 10 cm zip, and three hooks and bars. Each carried the full overskirt, 160 g, through twenty releases and a two-minute spin on a form; each release was timed and photographed at 3 m.
Results
The tape crackled audibly on release and caught the tulle. Two of four snaps popped on the spin. The zip took both hands and 40 seconds, and its pull showed as a lump. The hooks and bars held every spin, opened in eight seconds, and lay flat; the 15 cm overlap did not gape.
Conclusion
Only the hooks and bars were strong, fast and flat. As a result, the band closes with three hooks and bars and two snaps hold the overlap, drawn on page 6 and recorded on page 8. The full change timed at 48 seconds.

Experiment 9 · Closure through matte satin
Aim
To find the centre back closure that holds under the overskirt band and leaves no ridge or shine through the matte face of the crepe satin under stage light.
Method
Three 40 cm plackets were made in crepe satin over a satin facing: a lapped zip as the commercial pattern supplies (control), a hook and eye placket at 3 cm centres, and an invisible zip set with its own foot. Each was fastened twenty times, loaded to garment weight and photographed at 3 m under the hall lights with the band over it.
Results
The lapped zip held but its lap showed as a ridge the full length of the back and caught the light at the top. The hook and eye placket gaped between hooks under load. The invisible zip held, lay flat and showed as nothing more than a seam line.
Conclusion
The invisible zip was the only closure that disappeared into the seam. As a result, I removed the lapped zip from the pattern and used a 40 cm invisible zip with a hook at the neck, the modification recorded on page 6.

Trials & Proofs, Part IV
Fibre, yarn and fabric - performance against the end-use

This costume is three fibres doing three jobs, and almost every strength and weakness of the finished dress follows from that. The bodice, skirt and cuffs are polyester crepe satin: a smooth continuous filament, low in absorbency, woven in a satin weave with a crepe-twist back. Worn matte side out, the crepe face scatters light instead of reflecting it, which is why the dress read as black rather than grey or shine in Experiment 3. Polyester is also thermoplastic, so the princess seams press flat and stay flat, and the invisible zip sits in a seam that holds its line.

The tiers, the carrier and the sleeves are nylon tulle: a monofilament yarn knitted into a hexagonal net. The net has almost no weight and does not fray, because there is no woven edge to unravel, which is why every tulle edge in this dress is cut raw and left. Its stiffness comes from the yarn, not from a finish, so it survives washing; Experiment 1 showed that the mid weight stands off the body and settles in a second, which is the movement the design asked for. The same property is its weakness. Nylon holds heat and snags on anything sharp, and a torn net cannot be mended invisibly.

The collar is cotton piqué: a staple fibre spun into a yarn and woven in a dobby structure with a raised cord. Cotton is absorbent, takes a warm iron and stays white, which is why it beat poplin and satin in Experiment 2 and why the collar carries a different care label from the dress. The raised texture hides the interfacing, so the white reads as one flat shape from a distance, which is exactly the job it has.

Fabric construction decided as much as fibre. Polyester crepe, the same fibre as the crepe satin, puckered on a curved seam where the satin pressed flat; the difference was the weave, not the polymer. Soft, mid and stiff tulle are all nylon, and only one of them made a tier that could be worn. Two fabrics of identical fibre performed at opposite ends of the range in both experiments.

Against the end-use, the properties that fail are the ones the wearer feels rather than the ones the audience sees. Polyester and nylon have very low absorbency, so under hall lighting the dress will be warm and perspiration will sit on the skin. The sheer sleeves mitigate this by letting air through the net, and the collar, the only absorbent piece, sits where the heat is. Durability is a genuine strength: high tenacity and crease recovery mean the bodice, zip and skirt will outlast a natural-fibre equivalent, and the dress returns from storage with a press of the satin only.

The tulle was most at risk, so it was tested as its own variable: Experiment 4 showed it tears in a standard foot and feeds clean under a walking foot, and Experiment 7 showed a raw edge outlasts every finished one. If I repeated the project I would change one material decision. Nylon tulle was chosen for the tiers because it stands, and that was right, but a silk tulle for the sleeves alone would have given the same sheer line with far better comfort against the skin, and would have suited the 1926 reference (image 3) more honestly.
