The Sources, Gathered, Part I
The ledger. This folio documents a one-piece dance costume - a tea-length black dress with a detachable white collar - designed for a stage interpretation of Wednesday Addams at a school dance, and made to be read by an audience at performance distance rather than worn as everyday dress. The ledger is kept as an evidence trail: every feature claimed here is traced to a source on these pages, and every modification to a test.
1 Mourning dress, British, c. 1905. Read for the high fitted collar, the long sleeve and a bodice seamed close to the body.
2 Evening dress in black silk tulle, French, c. 1926. Read for tiered ruffles cut on the straight grain and set close together.
3 Party dress in black nylon tulle over taffeta, American, c. 1955. Read for the tea length and the full skirt over a fitted bodice.
4 Detachable white piqué collar on a black day dress, c. 1938. Read for the collar depth and how white reads against black at a distance.
Relevance to the focus area

The focus area of my Major Textiles Project is costume. Costume establishes a character before dialogue or action, and that idea has driven every decision in this folio.

The character is Wednesday Addams, dressed for a school dance she did not want to attend. The costume has to read at performance distance under gym lighting: one black silhouette, one hard white shape at the neck, and a skirt that moves when she does. It would not be worn as modern daywear, and that is the point.

The whole scheme is black on black in two textures - a matte satin bodice and sheer tulle tiers - so the audience reads shape and movement rather than colour. The single white collar is the only contrast, and it is detachable so the dress can be worn without it.

The fitted bodice, long sleeves, tiered skirt and white collar reference images 1 to 4 directly, so the audience recognises the period the character is drawn from before she has said a word.

Creativity and innovation

The innovation is a fusion of three periods in one garment: the closed, high-necked line of 1900s mourning dress, the tiered tulle of the 1920s, and the tea-length party silhouette of the 1950s, resolved for a character written in 2022.

The historical half of the fusion is structural - princess-seamed bodice, long fitted sleeve, graded tiers, tea length. The contemporary half is in the materials and the making - polyester crepe satin used matte side out, nylon tulle cut raw so the tiers stay light, and an invisible zip in place of a placket.

Two decisions are genuinely my own. First, the white collar is detachable on snaps, so one dress gives two readings: with the collar it is Wednesday, without it it is a black evening dress. Second, the three tiers are graded in depth, 12, 16 and 20 cm, so the skirt widens towards the hem and moves in a delay when she turns.

Selected pages of this folio print in black and white, in the manner of the 1964 television series the character comes from. Where a plate is monochrome, that is a design decision, not a printing fault.

The Sources, Gathered, Part II
Historical influence · dress in black, 1900 to 1955

My project draws on three moments when black dress was the height of fashion rather than its absence. The first is Edwardian mourning dress (image 1): a bodice seamed close to the body, a high collar and a long sleeve, worn in black for a fixed period after a death. I was drawn to how completely the silhouette is closed. Nothing is bare, and the whole figure reads as one dark shape.

The second is the 1920s tulle evening dress (image 2), where the same black is made to move: tiers of sheer ruffles set close together so the skirt lifts and settles a beat after the wearer. The third is the 1950s tea-length party dress (image 3), a fitted bodice over a full tulle skirt that stops above the ankle so the wearer can dance in it.

These three sources set the structure of my dress: the closed bodice and long sleeve from 1905, the tiered tulle from 1926, and the length and proportion from 1955. That layering shaped the manufacture as much as the look: the bodice and the tiers are constructed as separate layers on a satin underskirt and joined at the waist (page 8, step 9).

Screen influence · The Addams Family and Wednesday

The 1964 television series (image 6) was shot in black and white, and its costumes were designed to read in that range: matte black against mid grey against a hard white. That tonal discipline is the reason my dress has no colour at all, and the reason selected plates in this folio are printed in monochrome.

The 2022 series (image 7) is my strongest contemporary influence. Its school dance dress is black, tiered and ruffled, and it is built to move. I took the tiered silhouette and the idea that a black dress can carry all of its interest in texture and movement. The white collar is the character’s signature in the same series, worn every day; putting it on the dance dress as a detachable piece is my own decision, tested for attachment in Experiment 8.

Technique influence · jet beading and horsehair braid

Victorian mourning bodices carried jet beading (image 5), black on black, so the surface caught light without introducing colour. I use the same idea in a small way: a line of black glass beads along the cuff seam, the only surface decoration on the dress.

Horsehair braid inside a ruffle edge (image 8) is how a tulle tier is made to stand without stiffening the whole skirt. It set the manufacturing method for the satin underskirt hem and was tested against three other edge finishes in Experiment 7.

Critical analysis · plus, minus, interesting
Design Plus Minus Interesting
One
  • The white collar against black gives one clear point of emphasis.
  • Tea length and a full skirt let the wearer dance, which is the end-use.
  • Simple construction, so time is left for finishing.
  • A cap sleeve and a bow read as sweet, and the character is anything but. The mood is wrong.
  • One deep tier of tulle reads flat: no rhythm down the skirt.
  • A sewn-in collar cannot be removed, so the dress has one reading only.
  • The silhouette is historically accurate to 1955.
  • Black and white, no colour at all, holds the screen reference.
  • The bow gives the back a focal point the front lacks.
Two
  • The closed line and high neck are exactly the mood of the character.
  • Jet beading gives a black surface depth without colour.
  • Long sleeves add direction and elongate the figure.
  • A floor-length column cannot be danced in; the back slit only allows walking.
  • Beading the whole bodice by hand takes the whole term and leaves nothing for testing.
  • A stand collar in satin is hot under lights and hides the neck, so the white collar is lost.
  • Matte satin absorbs light where a shiny satin would flare.
  • The design is all texture and no shape change: it stands still well and moves badly.
  • A mourning dress at a dance is a deliberately contrasting idea.

Final justification

This is the best of the four designs because it keeps every element that succeeded in the earlier ideas and removes each one that failed. The closed line and matte black of Design Two are kept; the length and full skirt of Design One are kept; Design Three’s tiers are kept and graded so the skirt widens towards the hem and the eye is drawn down and out.

The flat belt answers the most repeated criticism in my analysis - that a bow reads as sweet - without losing the waist. The satin cuff finishes the sheer sleeve, and the single line of jet beads along its seam is the one place the surface catches light, a controlled version of Design Two’s beading. The invisible zip replaces the lapped zip of Designs Two and Three because a ridge shows through matte satin under stage light, a result confirmed in Experiment 9.

Against the end-use it does what a costume must: the black silhouette and hard white collar read as the character from the back of a hall, the graded tiers move when she dances, and the collar comes off on four snaps so the same dress serves a second scene. Nothing on it is decoration for its own sake; every feature is traced to a source on pages 1 and 2 or to a test on pages 9 to 11.

Critical analysis · plus, minus, interesting
Plus Minus Interesting
  • Two blacks, matte and sheer, give contrast within one hue, creating unity.
  • The white collar supplies one point of emphasis and nothing competes with it.
  • Graded tiers give the skirt direction and movement.
  • Raw-cut tulle snags on jewellery and chairs and cannot be mended invisibly.
  • Nylon tulle holds heat; the dress will be warm under lights.
  • Uncovered snaps show grey through a white collar.
  • The collar gives one dress two readings.
  • Three periods in one garment produce a deliberately unplaceable effect, which suits a character who is out of time.

The Making, Part I
Technical description

A tea-length black dance dress in 100% polyester crepe satin, worn matte side out, with long sleeves in black nylon tulle, a three-tier skirt in the same tulle over a crepe satin underskirt, and a detachable white collar in 100% cotton piqué.

The bodice is princess-seamed front and back with a round neckline faced in self fabric and finished with four covered snaps for the collar. The sleeves are sheer, set in, and end in a 4 cm satin cuff closed with a single covered button; a line of 4 mm black glass beads is stitched along the cuff seam. The skirt is three tiers of tulle, 12, 16 and 20 cm deep from waist to hem, each gathered at two and a half times its finished length and set to the underskirt at a stitched line; the tulle edges are cut raw. The underskirt is cut in four panels to 68 cm and hemmed over 25 mm horsehair braid. The dress closes at centre back with a 40 cm invisible zip and a hook at the neck. A 25 mm black grosgrain belt fastens at the left side with a hook and bar.

The collar is a flat piqué collar, 7 cm deep at centre back, interfaced, with a narrow stand that carries the four snap sockets. It is worn on the dress for the dance and removed for the scene that follows.

Patterns and modifications

The bodice and sleeve are from a commercial princess-seamed fitted-dress pattern. The three tiers, the underskirt and the collar are drafted from my measurements, because no commercial pattern carried tiers at the depths I wanted. Every change below is a modification I made to the purchased pieces, and each is drawn in the designated second colour on page 7. A full mock-up was made in calico before cutting, and the modifications were established on that toile.

Order of construction
Dress
  1. Trace the commercial bodice and sleeve; draft tiers, underskirt and collar.
  2. Sew a calico toile; fit and mark alterations.
  3. Alter the pattern: neckline +3 cm, waist shortened, sleeve narrowed.
  4. Lay out and cut satin and tulle; overlock all satin edges.
  5. Construct the bodice: princess seams, then side seams; press.
  6. Face the neckline; set four snap studs through the facing.
  7. Construct the underskirt; hem over horsehair braid.
  8. Set the three gathered tiers to the underskirt at the marked lines.
  9. Join bodice to skirt at the waist; insert the invisible zip.
  10. Set the sleeves; attach the cuffs and covered buttons.
  11. Bead the cuff seams through the cuff interfacing.
  12. Make the belt; hook and bar at the left side. Final press, satin only.
Tiers × 3
  1. Cut each tier across the full tulle width at 2.5 times the underskirt circumference; cut the lower edge on the finished line.
  2. Join the short ends in a plain seam, left raw.
  3. Zig zag over a gathering cord along the upper edge (Experiment 5).
  4. Draw up to the underskirt circumference; distribute evenly against the seam marks.
  5. Stitch to the underskirt with a walking foot (Experiment 4).
  6. Repeat for all three, deepest tier lowest.
Collar
  1. Cut two collars and two stands in piqué; cut one of each in sew-in interfacing.
  2. Baste interfacing to the under collar and the inner stand.
  3. Stitch the collar pieces right sides together; trim, clip and turn; press.
  4. Understitch the under collar.
  5. Sandwich the collar between the stands; stitch and turn.
  6. Set four snap sockets in the inner stand at the marks.
  7. Edge stitch the stand closed by hand.
  8. Press with a warm iron; snap to the dress and check the fall.

Trials & Proofs, Part I
Area one · Materials
Experiment 1 · Tulle weight for the tiers
Aim
To find the tulle that holds a gathered tier away from the underskirt, moves when the wearer turns, and does not scratch. Sample 1: soft nylon tulle. Sample 2: mid-weight nylon tulle. Sample 3: stiff bridal tulle.
Method
Three 100 × 16 cm strips were gathered to 40 cm and stitched to identical satin panels. Each was hung, photographed against a grid, spun by hand and photographed again at rest. Each was rubbed against the inside of a forearm for thirty seconds. Cost per metre was compared.
Results
Soft tulle collapsed against the satin and read as a fringe. Mid-weight tulle stood 4 cm off the panel, lifted on the spin and settled in about one second. Stiff tulle stood 9 cm off, did not settle, and left visible marks on the skin.
Conclusion
Only the mid-weight tulle gave a tier that stands, moves and can be worn against the arm. As a result, I selected mid-weight nylon tulle for the tiers and the sleeves and ordered 6 m, as costed on page 8.

Experiment 2 · Collar fabric: holding a white edge
Aim
To find the white fabric that holds a clean collar edge, stays white against black satin, and survives washing without the interfacing showing through.
Method
Three 12 cm collar samples were made by the same method in cotton poplin, cotton piqué and polyester satin, each with sew-in interfacing. Each was snapped to a black satin panel, photographed under a warm light, washed three times at 30 degrees, pressed and photographed again.
Results
Poplin held its edge when pressed but creased across the front by the third wash. Piqué held a crisp edge through all three washes and its texture hid the interfacing entirely. Satin read as grey under the light, showed the interfacing as a shadow and rolled at the edge.
Conclusion
Piqué was the only fabric that stayed white, flat and crisp. As a result, the collar is cotton piqué over sew-in interfacing, and it carries its own care label because it is a different fibre from the dress (page 7).

Experiment 3 · Bodice fabric under stage light
Aim
To find the black fabric that reads as black, not grey or shine, under the lighting of a school hall, and that takes a princess seam cleanly.
Method
Four 20 × 20 cm samples - crepe satin shiny side, crepe satin matte side, cotton sateen and polyester crepe - were pinned to a board and photographed at 3 m and 10 m under the hall lights with the camera set the same for each. A curved seam was machined in each and pressed.
Results
The shiny satin flared white along every fold at 3 m. Cotton sateen read as dark grey at 10 m. Polyester crepe read as black but the seam puckered. The matte face of the crepe satin read as black at both distances, showed the seam as a line and pressed flat.
Conclusion
Only the matte face of the crepe satin gave a true black and a clean seam. As a result, the bodice, underskirt, cuffs and belt backing are crepe satin worn matte side out, as costed on page 8, and the shiny face is used only inside the cuff.

Area two · Equipment
Experiment 4 · Presser foot for tulle over satin
Aim
To find the presser foot that feeds a gathered tulle tier over a satin underskirt without the top layer creeping ahead of the bottom or the tulle catching in the feed dogs.
Method
Three 40 cm tier seams were stitched with the same needle, thread and stitch length using the standard foot (control), a Teflon foot, and a walking foot. The start and end marks on both layers were measured for drift, and each seam was inspected for caught or torn tulle.
Results
The standard foot let the tulle creep 11 mm over 40 cm and caught it twice. The Teflon foot reduced the creep to 6 mm but still caught the tulle once. The walking foot fed both layers together with 1 mm of drift and no catching.
Conclusion
Only the walking foot fed tulle and satin as one. As a result, every tier seam and every sleeve seam is stitched with the walking foot, a step recorded on page 8.

Experiment 5 · Gathering method for the tiers
Aim
To find the method that gathers a 340 cm tulle tier to 136 cm evenly, without the thread breaking and without the gathers shifting while the tier is set.
Method
Three 100 cm strips of the selected tulle were gathered to 40 cm by two rows of machine basting (control), a ruffler foot set to one tuck in six, and a zig zag stitch worked over a cotton gathering cord. Each was measured at five points for evenness and then set to satin.
Results
The basting thread broke twice while drawing up. The ruffler gave even tucks but pleated rather than gathered and the tulle read as folded. The cord drew up without breaking, gathered evenly within 3 mm at every point, and held its place while the tier was stitched.
Conclusion
Zig zag over a cord was the only method that was even, strong and stable. As a result, all three tiers are gathered over cord, as recorded on page 8, and the cord is drawn out after the tier is set.

Experiment 6 · Marking tools on black fabric
Aim
To find the marking tool that shows on black satin and black tulle, survives handling, and leaves no trace after construction.
Method
Pattern lines were marked on 15 cm samples of both fabrics with white tailor’s chalk, a heat-erasable pen, tailor’s tacks in white thread, and a white water-soluble pencil. Each sample was handled for ten minutes, then brushed, pressed or sponged as the tool requires.
Results
Chalk showed clearly on satin and brushed out; on tulle it fell through the net in minutes. The heat pen marked satin but left a pale ghost after pressing. Tailor’s tacks held on the tulle through handling and pulled out clean. The pencil showed on satin but needed water to remove.
Conclusion
As a result, I marked the satin with chalk and the tulle with tailor’s tacks only, and kept the heat pen away from the satin, as recorded on page 8.

Experiment 7 · Hem and edge finish for tulle and underskirt
Aim
To find the edge finish for the tulle tiers that stays light and does not fray, and the hem for the satin underskirt that holds the lowest tier away from the legs.
Method
Four 20 cm tulle edges were finished raw cut (control), rolled hem, satin bound, and overlocked. Four satin hems were finished as a double-turned 2 cm hem (control), a 3 mm rolled hem, a 2 cm hem over 25 mm horsehair braid, and a faced hem. All were hung 48 hours with a tier over them and photographed against a grid.
Results
The raw tulle edge did not fray at all and stayed the lightest. The rolled hem fluted, the binding read as a hard line, and the overlocking curled. On the satin, the horsehair hem held the tier 5 cm out; the double turn and rolled hem let it collapse, and the faced hem creased.
Conclusion
As a result, every tulle edge is cut raw on the finished line, and the underskirt is hemmed over horsehair braid, both as specified in the allowances box on page 6.

Experiment 8 · Attaching the detachable collar
Aim
To find the fastening that holds the white collar flat on the neckline through a dance, comes off in under a minute, and shows nothing when the collar is on or off.
Method
Four collar samples were fastened to the same faced neckline sample with four small buttons and loops, four hooks and bars, a 12 cm strip of hook-and-loop tape, and four 10 mm covered snaps. Each was fastened and removed twenty times, worn on a form that was shaken for two minutes, and photographed with the collar on and off.
Results
The buttons showed as bumps through the piqué and took over a minute to undo. The hooks held but lifted the collar edge at each hook. The tape held flat but showed as a black strip when the collar was off and caught the tulle sleeves. The snaps held flat, released in seconds and left only four covered studs on the neckline.
Conclusion
Only the covered snaps were flat, fast and invisible both ways. As a result, the collar sits on four covered snaps set through the neckline facing, drawn on page 6 and recorded on page 8.

Experiment 9 · Closure through matte satin
Aim
To find the centre back closure that holds under the belt and leaves no ridge or shine through the matte face of the crepe satin under stage light.
Method
Three 40 cm plackets were made in crepe satin over a satin facing: a lapped zip as the commercial pattern supplies (control), a hook and eye placket at 3 cm centres, and an invisible zip set with its own foot. Each was fastened twenty times, loaded to garment weight and photographed at 3 m under the hall lights with a belt over it.
Results
The lapped zip held but its lap showed as a ridge the full length of the back and caught the light at the top. The hook and eye placket gaped between hooks under load. The invisible zip held, lay flat and showed as nothing more than a seam line.
Conclusion
The invisible zip was the only closure that disappeared into the seam. As a result, I removed the lapped zip from the pattern and used a 40 cm invisible zip with a hook at the neck, the modification recorded on page 6.

Trials & Proofs, Part IV
Fibre, yarn and fabric - performance against the end-use

This costume is three fibres doing three jobs, and almost every strength and weakness of the finished dress follows from that. The bodice, underskirt, cuffs and belt are polyester crepe satin: a smooth continuous filament, low in absorbency, woven in a satin weave with a crepe-twist back. Worn matte side out, the crepe face scatters light instead of reflecting it, which is why the dress read as black rather than grey or shine in Experiment 3. Polyester is also thermoplastic, so the princess seams press flat and stay flat, and the invisible zip sits in a seam that holds its line.

The tiers and sleeves are nylon tulle: a monofilament yarn knitted into a hexagonal net. The net has almost no weight and does not fray, because there is no woven edge to unravel, which is why every tulle edge in this dress is cut raw and left. Its stiffness comes from the yarn, not from a finish, so it survives washing; Experiment 1 showed that the mid weight stands off the body and settles in a second, which is the movement the design asked for. The same property is its weakness. Nylon holds heat and snags on anything sharp, and a torn net cannot be mended invisibly.

The collar is cotton piqué: a staple fibre spun into a yarn and woven in a dobby structure with a raised cord. Cotton is absorbent, takes a warm iron and stays white, which is why it beat poplin and satin in Experiment 2 and why the collar carries a different care label from the dress. The raised texture hides the interfacing, so the white reads as one flat shape from a distance, which is exactly the job it has.

Fabric construction decided as much as fibre. Polyester crepe, the same fibre as the crepe satin, puckered on a curved seam where the satin pressed flat; the difference was the weave, not the polymer. Soft, mid and stiff tulle are all nylon, and only one of them made a tier that could be worn. Two fabrics of identical fibre performed at opposite ends of the range in both experiments.

Against the end-use, the properties that fail are the ones the wearer feels rather than the ones the audience sees. Polyester and nylon have very low absorbency, so under hall lighting the dress will be warm and perspiration will sit on the skin. The sheer sleeves mitigate this by letting air through the net, and the collar, the only absorbent piece, sits where the heat is. Durability is a genuine strength: high tenacity and crease recovery mean the bodice, zip and underskirt will outlast a natural-fibre equivalent, and the dress returns from storage with a press of the satin only.

The tulle was most at risk, so it was tested as its own variable: Experiment 4 showed it tears in a standard foot and feeds clean under a walking foot, and Experiment 7 showed a raw edge outlasts every finished one. If I repeated the project I would change one material decision. Nylon tulle was chosen for the tiers because it stands, and that was right, but a silk tulle for the sleeves alone would have given the same sheer line with far better comfort against the skin, and would have suited the 1926 reference more honestly.
