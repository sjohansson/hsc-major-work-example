The Sources, Gathered, Part I
The ledger. This folio documents a two-scene dance costume - a black satin dress with sheer sleeves, a tiered tulle overskirt that hooks off at the waist, and a white collar that snaps on - designed for a stage interpretation of Wednesday Addams at a school dance, and made to be read by an audience at performance distance rather than worn as everyday dress. The ledger is kept as an evidence trail: every feature claimed here is traced to a source on these pages, and each making decision to a test or a recorded fitting.

2 Mourning dress, British, c. 1905. Read for the high fitted collar, the long sleeve and a bodice seamed close to the body. My composite silhouette study.
3 Evening dress in black silk tulle, French, c. 1926. Read for tiers cut on the straight grain, equal in depth and set close together. My tier study; contextual reference on page 2.
4 Party dress in black nylon tulle over taffeta, American, c. 1955. Read for the tea length and the full skirt over a fitted bodice. My proportion study.
Relevance to the focus area

The focus area of my Major Textiles Project is costume. Costume establishes a character before dialogue or action, and that idea has driven every decision in this folio.

The character is Wednesday Addams, dressed for a school dance she did not want to attend. The dress she wears to that dance in the series (image 1) is my starting point, and the audience will know it. On a stage it has to read at performance distance under gym lighting: one black silhouette and a skirt that moves when she does. It would not be worn as modern daywear, and that is the point.

The whole scheme is black on black in two textures - a matte satin dress and sheer tulle tiers - so the audience reads shape and movement rather than colour. That is the screen dress's own idea, and I kept it.

Images 2 to 4 are my comparisons with three periods of black dress. I use the closed line of the 1905 study, the repeated tiers of the 1926 study, and the skirt proportion of the 1955 study. These are my design choices, not claims about the screen designer's sources.

Creativity and innovation

The innovation is that the costume converts. The tiered skirt is a separate overskirt hung from a belt band that hooks at the side, and the white collar snaps on. Overskirt on and collar off is the dance dress. Overskirt off and collar on is the plain black dress the character wears every day in the same series. One garment serves two scenes, and the median change takes 48 seconds in the wings (Experiment 8).

My adaptation needs a quick change for a live audience. The carrier takes the tiers into their own band, so they can be removed without opening the dress. A flared carrier also clears the satin hem. The collar attaches separately; the dress remains finished in both scenes.

Three smaller decisions are also mine. The uneven tiers of the original are regularised and graded, 18, 26 and 34 cm, so the skirt widens towards the hem and moves in a delay when she turns. The ruffled cap sleeve becomes a long sheer sleeve with a satin cuff, closing the line as the 1905 dress does. The buckle goes, and the belt becomes the band that carries the overskirt.

The Sources, Gathered, Part II
Screen source · Wednesday and The Addams Family

The starting point is the dance dress from the series (image 1): a sheer black layer over an opaque underdress, a ruffled yoke over a black shirt collar, ruffled cap sleeves, a wide buckled belt, and four uneven sheer tiers to mid-calf. It was built for a camera at close range and for one scene. I kept what the audience needs to recognise her - the black on black, the tiers, the belt, the length - and changed what fails at ten metres or in the wings.

The white collar (image 7) is the character's uniform in the same series, worn every day. Putting it on the same dress as a detachable piece, with the tiers off, is what lets one costume play two scenes. The 1964 television series (image 6) was shot in black and white, and its costumes were designed to read in that range: matte black against mid grey against a hard white. That tonal discipline is the reason my dress has no colour at all, and the reason selected plates in this folio are printed in monochrome.

Historical influence · dress in black, 1905 to 1955

I compared three uses of black clothing, then drew the features I wanted in studies 2 to 4. The Met's 1902-1904 mourning dress links changes in permitted fabrics to stages of mourning. Its layered black textures suggested contrast without colour. I keep that restraint, but use a natural waist and sheer sleeves so the character can dance without a rigid period silhouette.

The Met records a silk evening dress from about 1926. My study 3 explores a dropped, straight line with repeated ruffles. I keep the moving layers but raise the waist and grade the tier depths; repetition alone looked static in Design Three. The 1955 study 4 instead concentrates volume below a fitted bodice. That proportion makes the waist legible from the hall and leaves the lower legs clear.

Black therefore serves different roles in my comparison: restraint, evening movement, and a shaped party silhouette. I combine selected features rather than reproduce a period dress. The overskirt, separate band, and detachable collar are the construction changes that make the combination work as a two-scene costume.

Technique influence · jet beading and horsehair braid

Victorian mourning bodices carried jet beading (image 5), black on black, so the surface caught light without introducing colour. I use the same idea in a small way: a line of black glass beads along the cuff seam, the only surface decoration on the dress.

Horsehair braid inside a ruffle edge (image 8) is how a tulle tier is made to stand without stiffening the whole skirt. It set the manufacturing method for the satin skirt hem and was tested against three other edge finishes in Experiment 7.

Critical analysis · plus, minus, interesting
Design Plus Minus Interesting
One
  • The silhouette identifies the character before she moves.
  • Tea length and a full tiered skirt let the wearer dance, which is the end-use.
  • Sheer over matte gives contrast within black.
  • A black collar on black vanishes at ten metres; the yoke ruffle fights it for the same spot.
  • The buckle is the one point of emphasis under hall light, and the wrong one; a cap sleeve reads as sweet.
  • Four uneven tiers have no rhythm, and a copy shows no development.
  • It was built for a camera at close range, not for a hall.
  • Black and white, no colour at all, holds the 1964 series and the new one equally.
  • The belt is the one hard line on a soft dress.
Two
  • The closed line and high neck suit the character.
  • Jet beading gives a black surface texture and depth without colour.
  • Long sleeves add vertical line and elongate the figure.
  • A floor-length column has line but no rhythm, and it cannot be danced in; the back slit only allows walking.
  • Beading the whole bodice leaves too little time for testing.
  • The stand collar can feel hot and obstructs the white collar.
  • Matte satin avoids the flare seen on shiny satin.
  • The design is all texture and no shape change: it stands still well and moves badly.
  • A mourning dress at a dance is a deliberately contrasting idea.

Final justification

Views 4A to 4C show the final design. The closed bodice and long sleeves retain Design Two's line; the moving tiers retain Design Three's rhythm. I grade the depths and flare the carrier to widen the skirt towards the hem. The buckle, sewn-on tiers, and fixed collar are removed. The numbered details on page 6 show the fastenings and bead line that are too small in these views.

The overskirt answers the fault the other three share: each is one dress for one scene. Hanging the tiers from a band (4B) means the same garment is the dance dress with the overskirt on, and the school dress with it off and the collar on (4C), in a median 48 seconds (Experiment 8). The band is the screen dress's belt with the buckle removed and a job given to it: it carries the tiers and hides the join. The satin cuff finishes the sheer sleeve, and its single line of jet beads is the one place the surface catches light, a controlled version of Design Two's beading. The invisible zip replaces the lapped zip because a ridge shows through matte satin under stage light (Experiment 9).

The final arrangement meets the quick-change target in three runs (Experiment 8). The collar supplies contrast in the school scene and the sheer tiers supply movement in the dance scene. I keep the heat and snagging limitations visible: a short trial does not prove all-night comfort. The three hooks carry the load; two overlap snaps control gaping. I inspect both before wear.

Critical analysis · plus, minus, interesting
Plus Minus Interesting
  • Two blacks, matte and sheer, give contrast within one hue, creating unity.
  • Graded tiers give the skirt direction and movement.
  • Two scenes from one garment.
  • Raw-cut tulle snags on jewellery and chairs and cannot be mended invisibly.
  • Nylon tulle holds heat; the dress will be warm under lights.
  • A damaged hook can weaken the closure. Check all three hooks and both overlap snaps before wear.
  • The band is all that survives of the original belt, and it is now the most functional piece on the dress.
  • The period studies give me a closed line, repeated tiers, and a defined waist. The combination remains a stage interpretation.

  2. Baste interfacing to undercollar and inner stand. Sew collar, trim, turn, and press.
  3. Understitch the undercollar. Sandwich its 38 cm neck edge between the stand layers.
  4. Set four sockets at 4, 14, 24, and 34 cm along the finished stand, measured from left front.
  5. Close the stand by hand. Warm press, snap onto the matching 38 cm neckline, and check the fall.

Area one - Materials

Experiment 1 - Tulle weight for movement

Aim
Choose a tier that stands off the skirt, settles after a turn, and is tolerable against an arm.
Method
I gathered three 100 x 16 cm strips to 40 cm on identical satin panels. After hanging for one hour, I read stand-off against a 1 cm grid. I turned each panel 180 degrees in one second, stopped it, and timed settling. I rubbed each over a forearm for 30 seconds.
Results
1.1 collapsed. 1.2 stood 4 cm out and settled in about one second. 1.3 stood 9 cm out, kept moving after the stop, and marked the skin. Prices were compared per metre.
Conclusion
I chose mid-weight nylon for the tiers and carrier. The short skin check also favoured it for sleeves, but does not establish all-night comfort. The 7 m order is on page 8.

Experiment 2 - Collar edge through washing

Aim
Choose a white collar fabric that retains its edge and hides the interfacing.
Method
I made three 12 cm collar sections with the same cotton sew-in interfacing and 6 mm seams. I photographed each on black satin under one warm lamp, then washed it three times at 30 degrees, gentle cycle. All line-dried; each was pressed at its labelled setting before comparison.
Results
2.1 poplin creased across the front by wash three. 2.2 piqué stayed crisp and hid the interfacing. 2.3 satin looked grey, showed the inner layer, and rolled at the edge. Table entries compare before and after washing.
Conclusion
I used cotton piqué and cotton interfacing for the detachable collar. Its separate cold-wash and warm-iron label is on page 7. A removable collar can be washed without wetting the beaded dress.

Experiment 3 - Black under hall lighting

Aim
Choose a black bodice fabric that remains dark at a distance and takes a curved seam.
Method
Four 20 x 20 cm samples shared one board under the same hall light. I photographed at 3 m and 10 m with fixed exposure and white balance. Each received the same curved seam, 70/10 needle, polyester thread, and 2.5 mm stitch. I pressed from the reverse at the fabric setting.
Results
The shiny face flared at 3 m; sateen looked grey at 10 m. Crepe stayed black but puckered at the seam. The satin matte face stayed black at both distances and pressed flat. Sample 3.1 is the control.
Conclusion
I selected the matte crepe-satin face for bodice, skirt, and cuffs (page 6). The shiny face is inside the cuff only. This comparison selects a fabric; it does not isolate weave from yarn or finish.

Experiment 4 - Feeding tulle tier seams

Aim
Find the foot that joins a gathered tulle tier to its tulle carrier without layer drift or catching.
Method
I stitched three 40 cm seams in the selected tulle with one 70/10 needle, polyester thread, and a 2.5 mm stitch. Start marks aligned. I used the standard, Teflon, and walking feet without pulling either layer, then measured the difference between the end marks.
Results
The standard foot drifted 11 mm and caught twice. Teflon drifted 6 mm and caught once. The walking foot drifted 1 mm and did not catch. Sample 4.4 records the caught area under the standard foot.
Conclusion
I used the walking foot for tier seams (page 8, overskirt step 5). Sleeve setting was checked on the toile as a separate construction decision; this straight tulle-to-tulle trial does not prove curved armhole performance.

Experiment 8 - Band closure and the change

Aim
Hold the 160 g overskirt securely, hide the fastening, and change scenes in under a minute.
Method
Four 40 mm bands used 10 cm hook-and-loop, four snaps, a 10 cm zip, or three hooks and bars. Each carried the overskirt through 20 releases and a two-minute spin on a form. I then added two overlap snaps to the hook band and checked the finished assembly in three wearer changes with one dresser.
Results
Tape caught the net and sounded loud. Two trial snaps opened during the spin. The zip needed two hands and 40 seconds. Hooks held, lay flat, and opened in eight seconds. The final hook-plus-snap assembly stayed closed during the wearer runs; changes took 52, 48, and 47 seconds.
Conclusion
I used three hooks and bars plus two overlap snaps (page 6). Median change time is 48 seconds. The snaps control gaping; the hooks carry the load. I check all fastenings before each wear; three runs do not establish service life.

Fibre, yarn, and fabric - performance against the end-use

I needed black that stayed dark under hall lights, tiers that moved, and a white collar that could be washed separately. The three materials solve different parts of that brief. Their weaknesses remain part of the evaluation.

The dress is polyester continuous-filament crepe satin, worn matte side out. Its surface stayed black at both viewing distances and its curved seam pressed flat in Experiment 3. The smooth face is inside the cuff. I used a cool reverse press because polyester is heat-sensitive; the trial does not establish permanent heat-setting of the seams.

Nylon monofilament forms the open tulle net. The mid-weight sample stood 4 cm out and settled in about one second (Experiment 1). Its structure lets the tier edge remain raw: no fraying appeared in the 48-hour hanging test (Experiment 7). That result does not make it snag-proof. Jewellery and damaged hooks can catch the loops, and a torn area would remain visible.

I chose the same tulle for sleeves after the short skin-contact check. That is limited evidence for prolonged wear. The open sleeve admits air, but the low-absorbency satin bodice can still feel warm. If I repeated the project, I would compare silk tulle for sleeves against nylon for skin comfort, snagging, and cost, while keeping the selected nylon in the tiers.

Cotton piqué uses staple yarns and a raised woven structure. In Experiment 2 its texture concealed the cotton sew-in interfacing and the edge stayed crisp through three washes. The separate collar takes the warm iron that the tulle cannot. Cotton still creases and can mark with perspiration, so I remove the collar to wash it and store it flat and dry.

Fibre alone did not predict the result. The satin and crepe samples were both polyester, yet one pressed flat and one puckered. Yarn, construction, and finish changed together, so this test selected the better fabric without proving which variable caused the difference. Similarly, the tulle trial compares three weights; it does not isolate stiffness due to yarn from stiffness due to finishing.

Equipment changed the making. The walking foot reduced tulle seam drift from 11 mm to 1 mm (Experiment 4). Cord held the 2.5:1 gathers in place (Experiment 5). Chalk came off the satin and tacks came out of the net (Experiment 6). These choices reduced handling of a surface that shows both heat marks and damaged loops.

The finished assembly used three hooks for load and two snaps to close the band overlap. The timed changes include both pieces and one dresser, with a median of 48 seconds (Experiment 8). This meets my one-minute target in those trials. Before another performance I still check the hooks, braid, beads, and raw net edges. The test duration is too short to claim lifetime reliability.

Final judgement

The final costume keeps the black silhouette and moving tiers that identify the dance scene, then changes to the white-collared school scene. The measured cut plan resolves the fit and clearance. Experiments decide the materials and methods; the design sequence explains the shape. Heat, snagging, and repeated fastening remain the limits I manage when the costume is worn.
